The 2030 Grid

Solve the dry year problem with renewables and batteries — not imported LNG.

In dry years, low hydro lakes force New Zealand to burn more fossil gas and risk blackouts. The standard response is to import Liquefied Natural Gas — expensive, slow to procure and shipped from overseas. This Blueprint shows a different answer: enough wind, solar and battery storage to keep the lights on through even the driest years, with energy that is cheap to run, fast to deploy and entirely locally produced.

▶ How the 2030 Blueprint works

The Blueprint simulates Aotearoa New Zealand's electricity system with the following generating capacity in place:

  • Hydro and geothermal retained — the backbone of NZ's renewable electricity, unchanged
  • 2.2–2.5 GW of wind — a significant expansion of the existing wind fleet
  • 3.2–3.5 GW of solar — equivalent to fewer than 4 × 500 W panels per NZ household; a modest ask per home that adds up to a major generation resource nationally
  • 1,000 MW / 4,000 MWh of battery storage — a 4-hour battery that does two jobs: shifting solar and wind generation into evening peaks and bridging short-duration dry and calm spells; and providing the inertia, frequency response and voltage support that spinning thermal generators in the North Island currently supply, allowing those gas plant to run far less while keeping the grid stable
  • No new gas — existing gas retained only as an emergency last resort
  • No coal

Dispatch order: hydro and geothermal run baseload, then wind and solar fill available demand. When supply exceeds demand, storage charges until full — then wind and solar are curtailed. Gas is the absolute last resort, used only when all other sources are insufficient.

Run-of-river hydro — approximately 25% of hydro generation is modelled as must-run (run-of-river), reflecting the constraints of South Island rivers that cannot be easily stored. This sets a floor on hydro output regardless of demand or supply conditions.

The assumptions are sufficient to show whether enough low-carbon electricity can be generated when it is needed. They are not a complete optimisation of cost or system design. Demand profiles are not changed.

Two cost measures are shown. The Levelised Cost of Energy (LCOE) is the lifetime build-and-run cost per MWh — it answers "what does this mix actually cost to build and operate?" The Wholesale Price estimate uses marginal pricing — the way electricity markets actually work. The last (most expensive) generator dispatched sets the spot price paid to all generators. Because renewables have near-zero fuel costs, they are dispatched first. In the Blueprint, gas is rarely needed, so it rarely sets the price — meaning wholesale electricity prices fall even though the capital cost of the system rises. This is the mechanism by which more renewables can lead to lower electricity bills.

The 2030 Blueprint is outlined in my book Decarbonising Electricity: Made Simple. For the full methodology, see: Crossland et al., Assessment of Electricity Decarbonization Scenarios for New Zealand and Great Britain, Energies 2020, 13, 2799. doi:10.3390/en13112799

Full LCOE assumptions — including source data, the gas load-factor formula, curtailment accounting, and the nuclear exclusion rationale — are documented in the About page methodology section.
