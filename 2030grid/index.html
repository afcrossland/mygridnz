The 2030 Grid

A simulation of what Aotearoa New Zealand's electricity could look like in 2030 — running against actual generation data. This is the 2030 Blueprint: a scenario for a 100% renewable electricity system that meets Aotearoa New Zealand's clean power goals.

▶ How the 2030 Blueprint works

To reach 100% renewable electricity I've made the following changes to the electricity mix:

  • Existing hydro and geothermal capacity retained (Aotearoa New Zealand's renewable backbone)
  • 3× the amount of wind generation
  • 3× the amount of solar generation (solar on every suitable rooftop)
  • No new gas; existing gas used only as an emergency last resort
  • No coal
  • Large-scale battery storage — enough to bridge low-wind, low-hydro periods

Dispatch order: hydro and geothermal run baseload, then wind and solar. When supply exceeds demand, storage charges until full — then wind and solar are curtailed. Gas is the absolute last resort, used only when all other sources are insufficient.

The assumptions are sufficient to show whether enough low carbon electricity can be generated when it is needed. They are not a complete optimisation of cost or carbon reduction. Demand profiles are not changed.

The 2030 Blueprint is outlined in my book Decarbonising Electricity: Made Simple. For the full methodology, see: Crossland et al., Assessment of Electricity Decarbonization Scenarios for New Zealand and Great Britain, Energies 2020, 13, 2799. doi:10.3390/en13112799

Full LCOE assumptions — including source data, the gas load-factor formula, curtailment accounting, and the nuclear exclusion rationale — are documented in the About page methodology section.

The 2030 Blueprint in action

The simulation runs the 2030 Blueprint mix against real-time weather and demand data over the last 28 days7 days, showing how an expanded fleet of wind, solar, hydro, geothermal and storage would actually perform. Gas almost disappears; storage charges during sunny and windy periods and discharges at night and during dry spells; curtailment rises when renewables generate more than demand and storage can absorb.
